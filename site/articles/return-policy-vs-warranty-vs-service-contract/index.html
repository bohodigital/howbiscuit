Return Policy vs. Warranty vs. Service Contract: What Actually Protects You?

A return policy governs whether the seller will take an item back, a warranty covers specified promises or defects, and a service contract is optional additional coverage sold separately. Read all three because they are not interchangeable.

Updated Aug 2, 2026 4 min read Researched Testing not applicable

What to know

  • Return policies usually handle early returns or exchanges under the seller's terms.
  • Warranties address specified product promises and defects, while service contracts sell additional repair or replacement coverage.
  • Compare the included warranty with the service contract's price, exclusions, deductibles, and claims process before buying.

A return policy, product warranty, and service contract protect against different problems.

  • Return policy: Whether the seller will accept the product back under stated conditions.
  • Warranty: A promise from the manufacturer or seller about the product or how covered defects will be handled.
  • Service contract: Optional repair or replacement coverage purchased separately, often marketed as an extended warranty.

Before paying for extra protection, read all three.

Return policies

A return policy determines whether you can return an item because you changed your mind, it does not fit, it failed to meet expectations, it arrived damaged, the seller sent the wrong product, or you discovered a problem shortly after purchase.

Important terms include the deadline, whether the item must be unopened, receipt and packaging requirements, restocking fees, return-shipping costs, refund method, and final-sale exclusions.

FTC guidance recommends checking these terms before buying online and preserving order confirmations, receipts, and communications. [1]

A generous return policy can resolve an immediate problem more easily than a warranty claim. Once that window closes, the seller may direct you to the manufacturer.

Warranties

A warranty is a promise concerning the product. A written warranty may specify covered components, duration, labor, repair or replacement options, shipping responsibility, exclusions, geographic limits, and the claims process.

Federal law generally requires written consumer-product warranties to be available for review before purchase when offered. Implied-warranty rights can also arise under state law, although their scope and duration vary. [2]

A warranty usually addresses defects or failures. It does not necessarily permit a return merely because you dislike the product.

Service contracts

A service contract is optional protection purchased in addition to the product. It may cover mechanical breakdown after the warranty ends, accidental damage, in-home service, replacement after repeated repairs, or certain labor and shipping costs.

It may also exclude the failure most likely to occur, duplicate the manufacturer’s warranty, impose deductibles, require specific repair providers, or offer only depreciated value.

FTC advises comparing a service contract with existing warranty coverage, expected repair costs, deductibles, exclusions, claims procedures, and the provider’s reputation. [3]

The three-day cancellation myth

There is no universal federal right to return every purchase within three days.

The FTC Cooling-Off Rule applies to certain sales made at a consumer’s home, workplace, temporary location, or similar off-premises setting. It does not create a blanket cancellation period for ordinary store or online purchases. [4]

Ordinary return rights usually depend on the seller’s policy, the contract, applicable state law, and whether the product was defective or misrepresented.

Which protection applies?

If you ordered the wrong color, start with the retailer’s return policy. If the product arrived broken, contact the retailer promptly because damaged-delivery procedures may be fastest. If a motor fails eight months later, review the manufacturer’s warranty. If the product fails after that warranty ends, check whether you purchased a service contract.

When a product did not match the seller’s description, preserve the listing, photographs, order confirmation, and communications.

Before buying a service contract

Ask what the included warranty already covers, when the service contract begins, whether coverage overlaps, whether accidental damage is included, what failures are excluded, whether there is a deductible or claim limit, who performs repairs, who pays shipping, what replacement quality is permitted, what happens if the administrator closes, and whether cancellation or transfer is allowed.

For an inexpensive product, the contract price may approach the cost of replacement. For an expensive item, the terms still matter more than the cashier’s tale of impending mechanical doom.

Keep a purchase file

Save the receipt, listing, serial number, warranty, service-contract terms, return-policy screenshot, delivery photographs, repair records, and correspondence. Policies can change after purchase, while a dated copy shows the terms presented when you bought the item.

Bottom line

A return policy helps undo or exchange a purchase within the seller’s rules. A warranty covers specified promises or defects. A service contract sells additional coverage.

Read the included protection first, then compare the extra plan’s price, exclusions, deductibles, and claims process with the actual cost and likelihood of repair.

Limits and cautions

  • State warranty and return rights vary.
  • This guide does not determine whether a specific dispute qualifies for a refund, repair, or chargeback.
