Should You Rinse Dishes Before Putting Them in the Dishwasher?

Usually, scrape food scraps into the trash or compost and skip the sink rinse. Pre-rinsing adds water and energy use. Use the dishwasher’s rinse feature only when dirty dishes must sit and the manual recommends it.

Updated Jul 31, 2026 4 min read Researched Not hands-on tested

What to know

  • Scrape off bones, seeds, peels, and large food pieces instead of rinsing every plate.
  • Run full loads without blocking spray arms or detergent dispensers.
  • Use detergent and a cycle appropriate for the soil level and water conditions.
  • Persistent residue may indicate loading, filter, spray-arm, detergent, water-temperature, or maintenance problems.

Most dishes do not need to be rinsed under the faucet before they go into a modern dishwasher. Scrape off large food scraps, load the racks correctly, add detergent, and let the machine wash them.

ENERGY STAR advises “scrape, don’t rinse” because hand-rinsing increases total water and energy use. It also notes that current dishwashers and detergents are designed to handle scraped dishes. [1]

What should be removed first?

Scrape or wipe away items that could clog the filter, block a spray arm, or remain intact through the cycle:

  • bones;
  • fruit pits;
  • corn cobs;
  • toothpicks;
  • paper labels;
  • thick piles of food;
  • large peels and seeds;
  • wax, candle residue, or non-food debris.

The goal is to remove solids, not to make every plate appear clean before the machine begins.

Why pre-rinsing wastes resources

A dishwasher uses a controlled amount of water for the load. Running the faucet over every item adds water before the measured wash cycle even starts. If hot water is used, it also adds water-heating energy.

EPA WaterSense similarly recommends scraping plates instead of rinsing and running full dishwasher loads. [2]

Will dirty dishes confuse the soil sensor?

Modern machines may use soil sensors to adjust cycle time or water use. The practical advice remains the same: load normally so the machine can assess the actual load. Completely washing dishes at the sink defeats the purpose of the dishwasher and may cause users to select a cycle based on an artificial condition.

The exact sensor logic differs by model, so follow the manual rather than relying on folklore about whether a plate must retain a ceremonial smear of sauce.

What if dishes sit overnight?

ENERGY STAR suggests using the dishwasher’s rinse feature when dirty dishes must sit overnight, noting that it uses a fraction of the water required for hand-rinsing. [1]

Many households can simply close the door and run a normal cycle later. Odor, dried starch, and machine design determine whether the rinse feature is useful.

Why are dishes coming out dirty?

Pre-rinsing may hide an underlying dishwasher problem. Check:

  1. Loading: Spray must reach the dirty surfaces.
  2. Spray arms: Make sure they rotate freely and their openings are clear.
  3. Filter: Clean it according to the manual.
  4. Detergent: Use fresh detergent and the recommended amount.
  5. Cycle: A quick or light cycle may not suit baked-on soil.
  6. Water: Hardness and incoming temperature affect performance.
  7. Dispenser: Tall items should not block it.
  8. Machine condition: A failing heater, pump, valve, or sensor can reduce cleaning.

Do not add more detergent indefinitely. Excess detergent can leave residue, especially in soft water.

Should pots and pans be treated differently?

Scrape them first. Soak or target truly burned-on material when necessary, but avoid using a full sink of running water as the default prewash. Check whether the item is dishwasher-safe and whether its coating or finish requires hand washing.

Cast iron, many knives, wood, insulated drinkware, and delicate decorated items may need different care.

Does a full load have to be crammed?

No. ENERGY STAR notes that dishwashers use about the same amount of energy and water regardless of how many dishes are inside, so full loads are more efficient. [1]

A full load still needs clear water paths. Nesting bowls tightly, stacking plates face-to-face, or blocking the spray arm can turn efficiency into an archaeological dig after the cycle.

Bottom line

Scrape dishes, do not routinely rinse them. Load the machine so water can reach every surface, run full loads, and use a suitable detergent and cycle. When results are poor, troubleshoot the dishwasher instead of performing most of its job at the sink first.

Limits and cautions

  • Manufacturer instructions take priority for a specific dishwasher.
  • Some unusual soils or long delays may require a rinse cycle or targeted treatment.
  • Cleaning performance depends on machine condition, detergent, loading, water hardness, and cycle selection.
