Why Do My Pipes Bang When a Faucet or Appliance Stops?

Water hammer occurs when moving water stops abruptly and creates a pressure wave. Quick-closing appliance valves are frequent triggers.

Updated Aug 5, 2026 4 min read Researched Not hands-on tested
Disclosure

This article contains no paid or affiliate links.

Read the disclosure

What to know

  • Water hammer occurs when moving water stops abruptly and creates a pressure wave. Quick-closing appliance valves are frequent triggers.
  • A bang exactly when water stops usually means water hammer.

Listen for the exact trigger.

A single sharp bang immediately after a:

  • Washing machine valve closes
  • Dishwasher stops filling
  • Ice maker fills
  • Faucet closes
  • Toilet valve shuts

is commonly water hammer.

Water hammer occurs when moving water is stopped rapidly, creating a pressure wave through the plumbing.

Distinguish the sound

Sharp bang at shutoff

Likely water hammer.

Rattling while water flows

Likely loose pipe supports or a pipe contacting framing.

Ticking as hot water starts or stops

Likely thermal expansion and contraction.

Repeated knocking from a water heater

Could involve sediment, thermal movement, check valves, or another appliance-specific issue.

Vibration from one faucet

Could involve a loose washer, cartridge, shutoff valve, or pressure issue.

Record the sound rather than labeling every plumbing noise water hammer by democratic vote.

Identify the triggering fixture

Test one fixture or appliance at a time.

Record:

  • Hot or cold side
  • Fixture
  • Time
  • Bang location
  • Whether sound occurs at opening or closing
  • Whether several rooms hear it
  • Whether plumbing work occurred recently

Quick-closing solenoid valves in washing machines, dishwashers, and ice makers commonly produce water hammer because they stop flow much faster than an ordinary hand-operated faucet.

Check visible piping

Inspect safely accessible pipes in:

  • Basement
  • Utility room
  • Under sink
  • Crawlspace entrance
  • Mechanical room

Look for movement when another person operates the fixture.

Do not place hands near:

  • Hot pipe
  • Moving appliance
  • Electrical equipment
  • Active leak
  • Unstable crawlspace

Loose pipes may need compatible supports that allow expected expansion.

Do not rigidly clamp every pipe to framing without understanding the pipe material and required movement.

Check household water pressure

A hose-thread pressure gauge can measure static pressure at a suitable outdoor faucet or laundry connection.

Follow the gauge instructions and turn every fixture off before reading.

Oatey recommends investigating static pressure above approximately 80 psi because excessive pressure can intensify water hammer and stress plumbing equipment.

Pressure requirements and plumbing codes vary. Use a plumber to evaluate:

  • Pressure-reducing valve
  • Expansion tank
  • Check valve
  • Backflow device
  • Municipal pressure
  • Well pressure switch

Water-hammer arresters

A water-hammer arrester contains a sealed compressible chamber that absorbs the pressure surge.

It should be:

  • Sized for the pipe and fixture
  • Installed near the quick-closing valve
  • Oriented according to its listing
  • Accessible where required
  • Compatible with local plumbing rules

Manufacturers commonly provide appliance-specific arresters for washing-machine hot and cold connections.

An arrester at the wrong location or size may have little effect.

Old air chambers

Older plumbing sometimes uses capped vertical pipe sections intended to trap air.

Over time, the air can dissolve into the water and leave the chamber waterlogged.

Draining the plumbing may temporarily restore an old air chamber, but repeated failure indicates that a modern listed arrester or another correction may be more appropriate.

Do not cut into concealed plumbing merely to recreate a century of plumbing folklore in drywall.

Thermal expansion can resemble hammer

In a closed plumbing system, heated water expands.

A check valve, backflow device, or pressure-reducing valve can prevent that expansion from moving back toward the utility supply.

Possible symptoms include:

  • Pressure rise after water heating
  • Relief-valve discharge
  • Pipe movement
  • Faucet surge

Have the expansion tank and system pressure evaluated when noises correlate with water-heater operation rather than valve shutoff.

Renters

Document:

  • Video or audio
  • Trigger
  • Room
  • Frequency
  • Visible pipe movement
  • Leak or wall damage
  • Date first reported

Report persistent banging to management.

Do not install arresters, modify valves, or open walls without authorization.

Stop and call a plumber when

  • A leak appears
  • Pipe movement is violent
  • Pressure repeatedly exceeds the expected range
  • Relief valve discharges
  • Noise follows plumbing renovation
  • Walls or ceilings become wet
  • Main shutoff or pressure regulator is involved
  • Pipes are inaccessible

Water hammer can damage fittings, valves, hoses, and appliances when severe or persistent.

Bottom line

A bang exactly when water stops usually means water hammer.

Identify the triggering valve, check pressure, secure loose piping correctly, and install a properly sized arrester near the source when appropriate.

The noise is a pressure wave. It is not the house expressing a preference for quieter laundry, though the timing makes the interpretation understandable.
