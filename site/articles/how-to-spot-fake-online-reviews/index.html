How to Spot a Fake Online Review

Treat review authenticity as a pattern-evaluation problem. Compare several sources, inspect timing and reviewer histories, look for specific recurring details and disclosed incentives, check whether the listing changed, and verify important claims against independent records.

Updated Aug 2, 2026 5 min read Researched Not hands-on tested
Disclosure

This article contains no paid or affiliate links.

Read the disclosure

What to know

  • A burst of reviews, repeated wording, or a blank profile can justify caution but does not independently prove deception.
  • Recurring specific details across multiple sources are usually more useful than generic praise or outrage.
  • A verified-purchase badge is one data point, not proof that the reviewer was uninfluenced or that the listing remained unchanged.

No single phrase, star rating, or reviewer badge proves that a review is genuine or fake. Treat authenticity as a pattern-evaluation problem:

  1. Compare reviews across multiple sources.
  2. Check when they appeared.
  3. Inspect reviewer histories.
  4. Look for specific product details.
  5. Identify incentives or insider relationships.
  6. Compare consequential claims with independent evidence.

The Federal Trade Commission recommends checking several sources and watching for a sudden burst of reviews over a short period, which can sometimes indicate manipulation. [1]

Start with the pattern, not one review

A single glowing or furious review may be real. Look for repeated signals across the review set.

Potential warning signs include many reviews posted within a narrow time window, unusually similar wording, generic praise that could describe any product, repeated marketing phrases, recently created accounts, accounts reviewing unrelated products in rapid succession, sharp rating changes after a promotion, reviews discussing features the product does not have, or clusters of ratings without written detail.

None of these proves fraud. A legitimate product launch, recall, viral post, software update, or widespread service failure can also produce a sudden burst.

Read the middle ratings

Two-, three-, and four-star reviews often reveal more tradeoffs than the extremes.

Look for recurring specific observations such as the same component failing, sizing running consistently small, a subscription requirement, difficult assembly, poor packaging, excellent performance under one condition but not another, reliable customer service, or a misleading product photograph.

A repeated specific issue across independent-looking reviewers is usually more informative than twenty variations of “Amazing product, highly recommend.” Specificity does not guarantee authenticity, but it gives you claims that can be checked against other reviews, specifications, and owner discussions.

Inspect the reviewer’s history

Ask whether the person reviews only one brand, whether dozens of reviews appeared on the same day, whether the account spans plausible products and time periods, whether wording remains identical across unrelated purchases, whether photographs appear original and relevant, and whether free products or other incentives are disclosed.

A blank profile is not proof of deception. Many genuine customers value privacy or rarely post reviews. The useful question is whether several suspicious features appear together.

Understand verified-purchase badges

A verified-purchase badge may indicate that the platform connected the reviewer to a transaction. It does not necessarily establish that the reviewer was uninfluenced, used the product extensively, was not reimbursed later, had no relationship with the seller, or reviewed the same version currently shown on the listing.

Treat verification as one useful data point rather than a certificate of moral purity issued by the internet.

Look for incentives and relationships

A review may be influenced by a free sample, reimbursement after posting, loyalty points, entry into a sweepstakes, employment by the seller, family or business relationships, or pressure to leave only a positive rating.

Disclosed incentives do not automatically make a review useless, but they change how much weight it deserves. An honest reviewer can receive a sample and still report useful details, while an undisclosed relationship can make apparently independent praise misleading.

The FTC’s Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule prohibits several deceptive practices, including buying or selling fake reviews, certain undisclosed insider reviews, review suppression, and incentives conditioned on positive or negative sentiment. The rule became effective on October 21, 2024. [2] [3]

Compare multiple types of evidence

Before a substantial purchase, compare reviews on more than one retailer, professional tests with disclosed methods, owner forums, repair discussions, warranty terms, recall databases, manufacturer specifications, return policies, and long-term reviews.

A review can report an experience, but it cannot replace a specification sheet, safety notice, warranty contract, or recall record.

Watch for review hijacking

A seller may substantially change a listing while retaining reviews from an earlier product. Check whether old reviews describe a different size, another color or model, unrelated features, packaging no longer shown, or an entirely different item.

Sort reviews by date and compare customer photographs with the current listing. Also check whether the product title or variation selector has accumulated unrelated models under one page.

Do not trust fake-review detectors blindly

Automated detectors can flag suspicious language, timing, or account patterns, but they do not have direct knowledge of every transaction or reviewer relationship. Use them as one signal, not a final verdict.

The most reliable approach is triangulation: several platforms, specifications, return terms, recall checks, and recurring detailed observations. A detector score is not a court judgment, despite the interface’s confidence and humanity’s tendency to assign moral authority to colored gauges.

Reporting suspicious reviews

Use the platform’s reporting mechanism when a review appears deceptive or undisclosed. Consumers can also report suspected fraud to the FTC. Avoid publicly accusing an identifiable reviewer or business of fraud without adequate evidence. [4]

Bottom line

Look for patterns rather than magic words. Compare sources, inspect timing and histories, prioritize recurring specific details, and check incentives and listing changes. Reviews are evidence, not revelation carved into a five-star tablet.

Limits and cautions

  • The article does not accuse any named reviewer, seller, platform, or business of fraud.
  • Automated fake-review detectors can identify patterns but cannot directly know every transaction or relationship.
  • Legitimate launches, recalls, promotions, and service failures can also produce sudden review bursts.
