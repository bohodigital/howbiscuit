How to Check a Used Refrigerator Before You Buy It

Verify the exact model and recall status, then make the refrigerator prove that it can hold 40°F or below and the freezer about 0°F. Inspect seals, wiring, cooling behavior, corrosion, water systems, dimensions, and total delivery and repair cost before paying.

Updated Aug 3, 2026 5 min read Researched Not hands-on tested

Read the disclosure

What to know

  • Measure refrigerator and freezer temperatures with appliance thermometers rather than judging by touch.
  • Reject damaged cords, burned wiring, heavy corrosion, chemical odor, pest contamination, and unresolved recalls.
  • Test the door gaskets, compressor and fans, water dispenser, ice maker, controls, and several interior zones.
  • Include delivery, installation, missing parts, energy use, immediate repair, and return risk in the real purchase price.

The direct answer

Do not buy a used refrigerator until you have verified:

  1. Exact manufacturer, model, and serial number.
  2. Recall status.
  3. Refrigerator temperature at or below 40°F.
  4. Freezer temperature near 0°F.
  5. Door-seal condition.
  6. Compressor and fan operation.
  7. Absence of electrical, corrosion, mold, pest, and refrigerant damage.
  8. Water-dispenser and ice-maker condition.
  9. Dimensions, hookups, and delivery route.
  10. Expected energy, repair, and transport cost.
  11. A written receipt and return agreement.

FDA recommends keeping a refrigerator at 40°F or below and a freezer at 0°F. Use appliance thermometers rather than trusting a control dial or a seller’s hand placed briefly inside the cabinet. [1]

Record the exact identity

Photograph the manufacturer, model, serial number, electrical rating, refrigerant label, manufacturing date when shown, and seller listing.

Similar-looking models can use different compressors, ice makers, control boards, filters, and recall remedies. A description such as French-door refrigerator is not enough to identify parts or safety history.

Check recalls

Search the Consumer Product Safety Commission recall database using the brand, complete model, serial range, and manufacturing date. [2]

Obtain proof that any required repair, replacement, refund, or software remedy was completed.

Do not buy a recalled unit based on the seller’s statement that it has never caused a problem. A recall concerns the product population and identified hazard, not the seller’s personal luck.

Test temperature properly

The refrigerator should be running normally before inspection.

Place appliance thermometers in the center of the refrigerator compartment and freezer. Allow enough time for temperatures to stabilize. A brief test immediately after plugging in an empty machine proves little.

Target:

  • Refrigerator: 40°F or below.
  • Freezer: about 0°F.

Check more than one refrigerator zone when possible because a blocked vent or failing fan can create warm areas.

Inspect the door gaskets

Look for tears, cracks, hardened rubber, mold, warping, gaps, loose corners, doors that drop or rub, and a French-door center flap that does not close.

Close a strip of paper in several points around the gasket. It should resist gentle pulling reasonably consistently.

A gasket may be replaceable, but check part availability and cost before purchasing.

Listen to the compressor and fans

Ordinary refrigerators produce compressor hum, fan noise, refrigerant movement, ice-maker sounds, and expansion clicks.

Warning patterns include repeated clicking without sustained cooling, grinding, screeching, heavy vibration, fans striking ice or plastic, continuous operation without reaching temperature, or a compressor that becomes extremely hot.

Open and close the doors and confirm the lights, switches, controls, and alarms behave normally.

Inspect the rear and bottom

Unplug the unit before touching or closely inspecting accessible rear components.

Look for burn marks, melted wiring, improvised splices, damaged cord, missing ground pin, oil residue, corrosion, pest droppings, nesting material, bent tubing, punctured cabinet, water damage, and a condenser packed with debris.

Do not touch refrigerant tubing, capacitors, terminals, or exposed wiring.

Check the interior

Inspect shelves, bins, drawers, vents, controls, light covers, drain channels, freezer panels, ice buildup, rust, cracks, persistent odor, mold, and insect evidence.

Replacement bins and shelves can be surprisingly expensive. Price every missing component before deciding that the appliance is a bargain.

Test water and ice systems

For a model with a dispenser or ice maker, confirm the water-line size and connector, no leakage, filter model and cost, dispenser operation, ice-bin condition, and absence of cracked tubing.

A used refrigerator should receive a new compatible certified filter and model-approved sanitation before drinking from it.

Compare energy use

Find the original EnergyGuide information or manufacturer specification and calculate the likely cost using the local electricity rate.

The FTC explains that EnergyGuide labels use standardized energy consumption and cost assumptions to help compare models. [3]

Do not compare old and new label dollar estimates directly without recalculating them using the same electricity price.

Measure the space and route

Record width, height, depth, door-opening clearance, hinge side, handle depth, ventilation clearance, water hookup, outlet location, hallway width, stair turns, elevator size, and building delivery rules.

Confirm whether doors must be removed for transport and whether qualified reassembly is available.

Include the full transaction cost

Add purchase price, delivery, stairs, door removal, disposal of the old unit, water-line installation, filter, missing shelves, cleaning, energy use, likely repair, and return risk.

A free refrigerator requiring a truck, two shelves, and a compressor repair has become economically creative.

Protect the transaction

Obtain a receipt listing the seller and buyer, date, price, brand, model, serial number, included parts, known defects, return period, warranty, and the seller’s representation that the appliance is theirs to sell.

Bottom line

Verify the model and recall status first, then make the refrigerator prove that it can hold safe temperatures.

Inspect seals, wiring, corrosion, cooling components, water systems, dimensions, and energy use before paying. A cold interior after ten minutes is not a complete appliance inspection.

Limits and cautions

  • A short test immediately after plugging in an empty refrigerator cannot establish stable temperature performance.
  • Age alone does not prove the condition or energy use of one exact appliance.
  • Refrigerant and electrical repairs require qualified service and model-specific procedures.
