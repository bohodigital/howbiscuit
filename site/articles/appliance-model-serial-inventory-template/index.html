Appliance Model and Serial Number Inventory Template

Create one record per appliance with the exact model and serial numbers, label photo, purchase and installation dates, seller, receipt, warranty, manual, service history, recall checks, and safe shutoff information. Store a backup away from the home.

Updated Aug 3, 2026 4 min read Researched Testing not applicable

What to know

  • A photograph of the rating label prevents transcription mistakes and preserves information that can fade or become inaccessible.
  • Model and serial numbers are needed for precise recall, warranty, parts, and service checks.
  • Do not publish serial numbers, access codes, Wi-Fi credentials, or full receipts in a public inventory.

The worst time to search for an appliance model number is after the refrigerator stops cooling, the washer leaks, or a recall notice appears. A simple inventory turns the label hidden behind the machine into information you can use immediately.

Create one row or page for each major appliance. Keep a working copy on your phone or computer and a backup somewhere outside the home or in a secure cloud account.

Room or location:

Exact model number:

Exact serial number:

Other identifiers: Product number, service number, type number, or manufacturing code

Rating-label photo: File name or secure link

Whole-appliance photo: File name or secure link

Purchase date:

Installation date:

Seller or installer:

Purchase price:

Receipt or invoice location:

Warranty provider and expiration:

Extended service contract: Provider, contract number, expiration, exclusions

Owner manual: File or official link

Consumables and parts: Filter, belt, bag, bulb, battery, tray, hose, or cartridge numbers

Electrical, gas, or water requirements: Copy from the rating label or manual

Normal shutoff location: User-accessible shutoff information only

Recall check date and result:

Service history: Date, company, work performed, parts, cost, and remaining warranty

Condition notes: Existing dents, corrosion, noise, leaks, or errors

Replacement date or disposal record:

Photograph the label instead of only typing it

Model numbers often contain similar letters, zeros, ones, hyphens, and spaces. A single transcription error can lead to the wrong part or a missed recall. Photograph the entire rating label in focus, then type the number into the inventory so it remains searchable.

Do not remove a safety label from the appliance. If it is behind a heavy machine, photograph it during installation or service rather than pulling the appliance out unsafely.

Common label locations include the refrigerator interior wall, washer door frame, dryer opening, dishwasher door edge, range drawer frame, microwave door frame or back, water-heater jacket, and the side or back of portable appliances. The manual may identify the exact location.

Use exact identifiers for recall checks

The Consumer Product Safety Commission recall database allows consumers to search products and follow official remedies. [1] A brand name alone is not enough because one recall may cover only particular models, serial ranges, or manufacturing dates.

Add a recall-check date when an appliance enters the home, when it changes owners, and when you hear about a relevant safety issue. Save the recall notice and remedy confirmation with the appliance record.

Do not resell or donate a recalled product without resolving the recall and confirming that transfer is lawful and safe.

Preserve purchase and warranty evidence

Attach or link the sales receipt, delivery record, installation invoice, warranty, and service contract. Record the seller and payment method without storing unnecessary full account numbers.

A manufacturer warranty, retailer return policy, and paid service contract are different documents. Note who administers each one, what dates apply, whether registration is required, and whether labor, parts, travel, removal, or installation are excluded.

Keep service receipts because a replaced part may have its own warranty. Record the technician’s diagnosis rather than only the amount paid.

Include insurance-ready photos

The National Association of Insurance Commissioners recommends creating a home inventory with descriptions, photographs or video, serial numbers, receipts when available, and an off-site copy. [2]

Take a wide photo showing the appliance in its room and a close photo of the label. For built-in appliances, also retain installation invoices and any permitted information about cabinetry or utility connections.

An inventory documents what you owned; it does not guarantee that an insurer will cover a loss or pay the amount recorded.

Protect sensitive information

Serial numbers and receipts can be useful to thieves or social engineers. Keep the complete inventory private. Do not post label photos publicly when asking for repair advice.

Redact addresses, order numbers, payment details, access codes, QR codes, and Wi-Fi credentials before sharing a record with anyone who does not need them. Use a password-protected or encrypted location for the master copy.

Update the record after every change

When an appliance is repaired, replaced, recalled, moved, or sold, update the inventory immediately. Mark the old record as retired rather than deleting it; prior model and service information can matter for insurance, tax, landlord, or warranty questions.

Set one annual reminder to verify that links still work, warranty dates are accurate, and the backup can actually be opened.

Minimal spreadsheet header

For a compact version, start with this line:

Location | Appliance | Brand | Model | Serial | Label photo | Purchase date | Warranty end | Manual | Recall checked | Last service | Notes

The inventory does not need to be elaborate. It needs to be exact, private, backed up, and available before the appliance is inaccessible.

Limits and cautions

  • Label locations, identifiers, warranty terms, registration requirements, and service records vary by manufacturer and appliance.
