Should You Wash Raw Chicken?

No. USDA advises against washing raw chicken because splashing can spread bacteria around the sink and nearby surfaces.

Updated Aug 1, 2026 3 min read Researched Not hands-on tested
Disclosure

This article contains no paid or affiliate links.

Read the disclosure

What to know

  • Do not rinse raw chicken in the sink.
  • Prevent cross-contamination by separating raw poultry and cleaning hands, tools, and surfaces.
  • Cook poultry to a food-thermometer reading of 165°F in the appropriate location.

On this page

  1. Why rinsing can make the kitchen less safe
  2. A safer preparation sequence
  3. What if you already rinsed it?
  4. What about feathers, bone fragments, or packaging liquid?
  5. Limits of this guidance

Do not wash raw chicken before cooking it. USDA advises against washing raw poultry because water can splash bacteria from the chicken onto the sink, counters, utensils, other food, and nearby surfaces. Cooking—not rinsing—is the control used to destroy harmful bacteria in the meat. [1]

The diagram shows the safer workflow: keep raw poultry out of the rinse stream, contain its juices, clean what it touched, and verify cooking with a thermometer.

Why rinsing can make the kitchen less safe

A faucet striking chicken or a bowl of water creates droplets that are not confined to the sink basin. You may not see them land on a faucet handle, drying rack, produce, sponge, or counter. USDA calls this cross-contamination and notes that washing poultry can spread bacteria rather than remove the hazard. [2]

Salt water, vinegar, or lemon juice does not turn rinsing into a validated kill step. USDA says washing, rinsing, or brining meat and poultry in those solutions does not destroy bacteria. [2] A marinade can be useful for flavor, but it should be handled as raw-poultry contact and not treated as a disinfectant.

A safer preparation sequence

  1. Clear ready-to-eat food and clean dishes away from the work area.
  2. Open the package carefully so juices stay contained.
  3. Transfer the chicken directly to the cooking vessel or preparation surface. If a recipe calls for drying the surface, use disposable paper towel cautiously and discard it immediately without wiping other surfaces.
  4. Wash hands with soap and water after touching the raw poultry or its packaging.
  5. Clean and sanitize the cutting board, utensils, counter, sink contact points, and handles that were touched.
  6. Cook, then use a food thermometer rather than color alone.

USDA states that all poultry should reach a safe minimum internal temperature of 165°F as measured with a food thermometer. [2] Follow USDA instructions for thermometer placement in the specific cut or whole bird; touching bone or measuring only a thin edge can mislead.

What if you already rinsed it?

Do not panic and do not rinse again. Move the chicken to cooking, keep other food away, wash your hands, then clean and sanitize the sink and the surrounding area that could have been splashed. Replace or launder a contaminated towel rather than using it on clean dishes. If raw juices reached ready-to-eat food, discard that food. [2]

The goal is not a visibly perfect kitchen. It is breaking the path from raw poultry to mouths, clean utensils, and foods that will not receive further cooking.

What about feathers, bone fragments, or packaging liquid?

If a product has a foreign object or appears abnormal, follow the producer’s handling guidance or return/discard it; rinsing is not a reliable correction. Packaging liquid is not evidence that the chicken needs a bath. Drain or blot only when a recipe requires it, and treat everything that contacts the liquid as contaminated until cleaned.

Limits of this guidance

This article summarizes federal household guidance and reports no kitchen experiment. It cannot assess your chicken’s storage history, a medical vulnerability, a boil-water notice, or local outbreak advice. People at higher risk of foodborne illness may need additional precautions. Current public-health instructions and the product’s safe-handling label take priority for a specific event.

Limits and cautions

  • This is U.S. household food-safety guidance and does not replace local public-health instructions.
  • The article does not evaluate a particular piece of poultry or kitchen.
  • No washing or cooking method was evaluated for this article.
