Should You Wash Raw Chicken Before Cooking It?

Do not wash raw chicken. Rinsing can spread bacteria to the sink, faucet, counters, utensils, and nearby food. Handle poultry carefully and cook the thickest part to at least 165°F, or 74°C, measured with a food thermometer.

What to know

  • Rinsing poultry creates a cross-contamination risk and does not make it safe.
  • Cook chicken to 165°F in the thickest part rather than judging by color or clear juices.
  • Clean and sanitize surfaces that contacted raw poultry, and never mix household cleaners.

Do not wash raw chicken before cooking it.

Rinsing does not make poultry safe. It can splash bacteria onto the sink, faucet, counters, utensils, clothing, and nearby food. Proper cooking is what destroys harmful bacteria in the chicken. USDA and CDC guidance advises consumers not to wash raw poultry. [1] [2]

What to do instead

Keep raw chicken separate from ready-to-eat food. Remove it from the package without splashing liquid and place it directly in the cooking vessel or on a dedicated preparation surface. Wash your hands with soap and water after touching the chicken or packaging. Clean and sanitize contaminated surfaces, and cook the thickest part to at least 165°F, or 74°C, with a food thermometer.

Do not judge safety by color alone. Browning, clear juices, and texture are not substitutes for an internal-temperature reading.

Why washing spreads bacteria

Water striking raw chicken and the sink can create droplets that carry bacteria beyond the basin. Cross-contamination can then happen when someone touches the faucet, prepares salad on the same counter, sets a clean plate near the sink, uses a contaminated towel, or handles seasoning containers with unwashed hands.

USDA identifies poultry washing as an unnecessary cross-contamination risk. [3]

What about vinegar, lemon juice, or salt water?

Soaking or rinsing chicken in vinegar, citrus juice, salt water, or another household mixture does not replace safe handling and cooking. A marinade may change flavor or texture, but it should not be treated as a disinfecting process.

Marinate chicken in the refrigerator, not on the counter. Discard marinade that contacted raw poultry unless a recipe safely brings it to a boil.

What if the chicken feels slimy?

Packaged raw chicken may have surface moisture. You do not need to rinse it away.

If a recipe benefits from a drier surface for browning, pat the chicken with a disposable paper towel and discard the towel immediately. Wash your hands and sanitize anything the towel or chicken touched. Do not use an ordinary reusable kitchen towel unless it will be handled as contaminated laundry.

A strong unpleasant odor, damaged packaging, unusual discoloration, or storage beyond safe limits may indicate spoilage. Washing cannot restore spoiled chicken.

If you already washed it

There is no need for panic or demolition of the sink. Move ready-to-eat food away, wash your hands, clean the sink, faucet, handles, nearby counter, and contacted utensils with hot soapy water, then apply an appropriate kitchen sanitizer according to its label. Use fresh towels or disposable paper products and wash contaminated cloths thoroughly.

Never mix bleach with ammonia, vinegar, acids, or other cleaners. Dangerous gases can result. [4]

Use the thermometer correctly

Insert the probe into the thickest part without touching bone. For a whole chicken, check the innermost thigh and wing and the thickest part of the breast. For pieces, check the thickest section of the largest piece.

USDA and CDC specify 165°F as the safe minimum internal temperature for poultry. [3] [2]

Clean the thermometer probe after use according to its instructions.

Prevent cross-contamination

Store raw chicken on the lowest refrigerator shelf in a leak-resistant container. Use a separate cutting board or clean the board thoroughly before another use. Do not reuse a plate that held raw chicken. Avoid touching spice jars with contaminated hands. Refrigerate leftovers promptly. Replace damaged cutting boards that cannot be cleaned effectively.

Bottom line

Do not wash raw chicken.

Keep it separate, minimize contact with surrounding surfaces, wash your hands, clean contaminated equipment, and verify 165°F with a food thermometer. Water cannot accomplish what heat and sound kitchen hygiene do.

Limits and cautions

  • The guide addresses ordinary home preparation and does not replace instructions for a regulated commercial kitchen.
  • Smell, color, and texture cannot establish that cooked poultry has reached a safe internal temperature.
