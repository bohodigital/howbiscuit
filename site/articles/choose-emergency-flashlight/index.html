How to Choose an Emergency Flashlight That Will Still Work When the Power Fails

Emergency lighting needs reliable low and medium modes more than a brief turbo output. Standardized ANSI/PLATO FL 1 claims make output, runtime, beam distance, impact, and water resistance easier to compare.

Updated Aug 5, 2026 3 min read Researched Not hands-on tested
Disclosure

This article contains no paid or affiliate links.

Read the disclosure

What to know

  • Emergency lighting needs reliable low and medium modes more than a brief turbo output. Standardized ANSI/PLATO FL 1 claims make output, runtime, beam distance, impact, and water resistance easier to compare.
  • Choose standardized output, useful low-mode runtime, a maintainable battery system, water resistance, and simple controls.

Buy at least one flashlight with:

  • Standardized performance claims
  • Low and medium modes
  • Common or replaceable battery
  • Physical switch
  • Water resistance
  • Impact resistance
  • Battery-state indication
  • Assigned storage location

Emergency kits should include flashlights and spare batteries.

Use ANSI/PLATO FL 1 specifications

The current FL 1 standard defines methods for reporting:

  • Light output
  • Beam distance
  • Peak beam intensity
  • Runtime
  • Impact resistance
  • Water and dust protection

A product claiming compliance should show recognizable symbols or values tied to those tests.

That does not prove excellent quality. It makes the claims more comparable than uncontrolled phrases such as “military sun cannon.”

Lumens

Lumens measure total light output.

More lumens help with:

  • Outdoor search
  • Large rooms
  • Inspection
  • Signaling

Lower modes are better for:

  • Reading
  • Walking indoors
  • Preserving battery
  • Avoiding glare

A brief maximum mode can drain a battery quickly or reduce output as the light heats.

Choose a useful medium mode, not merely a spectacular startup number.

Candela and beam distance

Candela describes beam intensity.

A focused beam reaches farther than a broad flood beam at the same lumen output.

Choose:

  • Flood beam for rooms, repairs, and walking
  • Focused beam for distant observation and signaling
  • Adjustable beam for flexibility, with more moving parts

Reported beam distance is a standardized visibility threshold, not necessarily the distance at which a hazard can be identified clearly.

Runtime

Check runtime for every mode.

Runtime testing continues until output falls to a defined endpoint, so the light may be much dimmer near the reported end than at startup.

For emergency use, prefer:

  • Several hours on medium
  • Long low-mode runtime
  • Predictable step-down
  • Battery warning

Battery options

Replaceable alkaline

Advantages:

  • Widely available
  • Easy to store separately
  • Simple replacement

Tradeoffs:

  • Leakage risk
  • Lower performance in some high-output lights
  • Waste

Inspect stored batteries regularly.

Primary lithium

Advantages:

  • Long shelf life
  • Better cold performance
  • Lower leakage risk

Tradeoffs:

  • Higher cost
  • Compatibility required

Rechargeable lithium-ion

Advantages:

  • High energy
  • Reusable
  • Strong output

Tradeoffs:

  • Needs charging
  • Ages during storage
  • Heat sensitivity
  • Replacement may be proprietary

Keep a charging plan that does not depend solely on household electricity.

Water and impact resistance

Choose water resistance for the expected environment.

A rain-resistant light is not necessarily safe for immersion.

Impact claims apply to test conditions, not every drop onto stairs, concrete, or a flooded basement.

Controls

Emergency-friendly controls include:

  • Simple physical switch
  • Direct access to low or medium
  • Lockout for storage
  • No mandatory application
  • No complicated sequence
  • Glove usability

A flashlight requiring seven taps to avoid strobe mode is less a tool than a small user-interface dispute.

Store and maintain it

Keep flashlights beside beds, near exits, in emergency kits, and in vehicles where temperature limits allow.

Every three to six months:

  1. Test every mode.
  2. Inspect battery contacts.
  3. Recharge supported batteries.
  4. Replace leaking or expired cells.
  5. Confirm spare batteries fit.
  6. Clean the lens.
  7. Return the light to its assigned location.

Use flashlights instead of candles

Flashlights avoid the open-flame risk that candles add during an outage.

Keep a headlamp as a useful second layer for hands-free work.

Bottom line

Choose standardized output, useful low-mode runtime, a maintainable battery system, water resistance, and simple controls.

The best emergency flashlight is not the brightest object advertised online. It is the one where expected, containing a healthy battery, and turning on before someone begins locating candles beside a gas appliance.
