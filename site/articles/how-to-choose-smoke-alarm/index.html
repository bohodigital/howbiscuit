How to Choose a Smoke Alarm

Buy enough traceable UL 217-certified alarms for every sleeping room, outside each separate sleeping area, and every level of the home. Prefer interconnection, appropriate sensing coverage, battery backup, and alerts accessible to every occupant.

Updated Aug 3, 2026 5 min read Researched Not hands-on tested

Read the disclosure

What to know

  • One premium alarm cannot replace several correctly located alarms.
  • Verify the exact model and certification record rather than relying on a logo in marketplace artwork.
  • Interconnected alarms spread a warning throughout the home when one unit detects smoke.
  • Test monthly and replace alarms by the manufacturer’s required age, commonly ten years.

The direct answer

Choose smoke alarms that are certified to the applicable UL 217 requirements, come from an identifiable manufacturer, can be verified in the certification laboratory’s directory, interconnect where possible, use backup power where household power is involved, and provide alerts accessible to everyone in the home.

Then buy enough units for every sleeping room, outside each separate sleeping area, and every level of the home, including the basement. The U.S. Fire Administration recommends those locations. [1]

Verify UL 217 certification

UL 217 is the principal U.S. safety standard for residential smoke alarms. Current editions include performance tests intended to improve fire detection and resistance to nuisance alarms. [2]

Do not rely only on wording such as “meets UL standards,” “UL tested,” a logo shown in marketplace artwork, or a certificate belonging to another model.

Record the manufacturer, exact model, certification mark, file or control number, production date, and replacement date. Verify the product in the certification body’s official directory.

Buy enough alarms before comparing features

Draw the home’s layout and mark every bedroom, every separate sleeping area, every floor, the basement, existing hardwired locations, and occupants who need visual, tactile, voice, or lower-frequency alerts.

One expensive alarm in a hallway cannot replace required alarms inside bedrooms or on other floors.

Sensor coverage

Traditional residential smoke alarms have commonly used ionization or photoelectric sensing.

Ionization technology generally responds more readily to rapidly flaming fires, while photoelectric technology generally responds more readily to smoldering fires. Current product designs can also use multi-criteria sensing under newer UL 217 requirements.

Choose alarms and a placement plan that provide broad detection coverage and comply with current code and manufacturer instructions.

Do not remove one alarm type simply because another location produces fewer nuisance alarms.

Interconnected alarms

Interconnection means that when one alarm detects smoke, every connected alarm sounds.

This is especially important when fire starts in a basement, bedroom doors are closed, the home has several levels, or occupants sleep far from the origin.

Interconnection can be hardwired, wireless, or part of a compatible alarm network. Confirm that every model belongs to the same approved system. Unrelated smart alarms do not necessarily interconnect merely because they all use Wi-Fi.

Power options

Replaceable-battery alarms allow flexible installation but require battery maintenance and may be prohibited in some retrofit applications by local law.

Sealed long-life batteries avoid routine battery replacement, but the entire alarm must be replaced at the end of its service life. The alarm still requires monthly testing.

Hardwired alarms use household power and normally require backup power during an outage. Installation and interconnection must match the home wiring and local code.

Accessibility

Standard audible alarms may not wake or warn everyone.

Consider strobe lights, bed or pillow shakers, voice alerts, spoken location, low-frequency sounders, remote annunciation, and large test buttons.

USFA recommends specialized visual and tactile alert devices for people who are deaf or hard of hearing. [1]

A smartphone notification can supplement the local alarm but should not be the only warning.

Combination smoke and carbon monoxide alarms

A combination unit can reduce the number of devices, but both functions must be verified.

Look for the applicable certification for smoke and carbon monoxide detection, distinct warning patterns or voice announcements, placement acceptable for both functions, and compatible interconnection.

Follow the exact manual because the ideal smoke-alarm location is not automatically the ideal location for every carbon monoxide alarm.

Manufacturing and replacement dates

Smoke alarms do not last indefinitely.

USFA recommends replacing smoke alarms when they are ten years old or sooner when the manufacturer requires it or the alarm fails testing. [1]

Before buying, check the manufacturing date on the actual unit and avoid old inventory.

A ten-year battery installed in a nine-year-old alarm does not make the sensor new.

Nuisance alarms

Do not solve cooking alarms by removing or disabling the detector.

Follow the model’s placement instructions, choose a current product designed to resist ordinary cooking nuisance alarms, use a hush feature only as directed, improve kitchen exhaust, keep the alarm clean, and relocate it only when permitted by code and the manufacturer.

Check recalls

Search the CPSC database using the manufacturer and exact model before installation. Keep the receipt and register the product when appropriate.

Do not install or resell an alarm with an unresolved safety recall.

Test after installation

Follow the exact manual. General federal guidance recommends monthly testing, battery maintenance according to the alarm design, replacement by the required age, and a practiced household escape plan. [1]

The test button checks the electronics and sounder. It does not recreate every real fire condition.

Bottom line

Buy a traceable UL 217-certified system, not one lonely alarm.

Install alarms in every bedroom, outside sleeping areas, and on every level. Interconnect them whenever possible, match accessibility and power to the household, check recalls, test monthly, and replace each alarm at its required age.

Limits and cautions

  • Local fire and building codes can require additional locations, hardwiring, sealed batteries, or specific alarm types.
  • Smartphone notifications supplement rather than replace a locally sounding alarm.
  • Combination smoke and carbon monoxide alarms must meet the applicable requirements for both functions.
