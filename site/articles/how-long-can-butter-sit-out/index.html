How Long Can Butter Sit Out?

USDA FoodKeeper data says ordinary butter may be left at room temperature for one to two days. Keep only a small covered portion out and refrigerate the rest.

Updated Aug 3, 2026 4 min read Researched Not hands-on tested
Disclosure

What to know

  • Keep counter butter in a clean covered dish away from sunlight, stove heat, pets, insects, and used utensils.
  • Leave out only the amount expected to be used within one or two days.
  • Whipped, reduced-fat, homemade, raw-milk, and flavored butter can require stricter refrigeration.
  • Discard butter with mold, contamination, or clearly rancid or abnormal odor.

The direct answer

Ordinary commercial butter may be left at room temperature for one to two days, according to USDA FoodKeeper data. [1]

Keep only the amount you expect to use during that period in a clean, covered dish. Refrigerate the remaining butter.

This guidance does not automatically apply to whipped butter, reduced-fat spreads, homemade butter, raw-milk butter, or compound butter containing garlic, herbs, cheese, meat, fruit, or another perishable ingredient.

Why butter is more stable than milk

Butter contains much less water and substantially more fat than fluid milk. Those conditions make ordinary butter less supportive of rapid microbial growth than milk, cream, or soft dairy products.

That does not make butter indefinitely shelf-stable. Air, warmth, light, contamination, and time can still make it rancid or otherwise unacceptable.

Federal power-outage guidance treats butter differently from milk, yogurt, eggs, meat, and leftovers, which reflects its greater storage stability. [2]

Use a covered container

Store counter butter in a clean butter dish with a lid or another food-safe covered container.

Protect it from:

  • Direct sunlight.
  • Stove and oven heat.
  • Steam.
  • Pets and insects.
  • Dust.
  • Used utensils.
  • Strong household odors.

An open decorative tray may look like breakfast in a farmhouse, but it also leaves the butter exposed to the actual kitchen.

Leave out a small portion

A practical routine is:

  1. Cut off one or two days of expected use.
  2. Place it in the covered dish.
  3. Refrigerate the remaining butter in its wrapper or a sealed container.
  4. Clean the dish before refilling it.

Do not repeatedly combine fresh butter with old residue.

Salted versus unsalted butter

Salted butter can resist quality deterioration somewhat better than unsalted butter, but the one-to-two-day FoodKeeper period remains a useful conservative household rule for either.

Unsalted butter being preferred for baking does not make the entire month’s supply a countertop product.

Room temperature matters

A cool kitchen differs from a hot summer apartment, sunny windowsill, top of a refrigerator, counter beside an oven, outdoor picnic, or home without climate control during a heat wave.

Refrigerate butter when it becomes oily, separates, melts, or develops an abnormal odor quickly.

Butter crocks

A water-sealed butter crock can reduce exposure to air when maintained correctly. It does not override the butter manufacturer’s instructions, the one-to-two-day USDA guidance, warm-room conditions, contamination from utensils, or the need to clean the vessel.

Do not keep butter for weeks merely because its container has acquired a vaguely European name.

Flavored and compound butter

Refrigerate butter containing fresh garlic, herbs, cheese, seafood, meat, fruit, or vegetables.

Garlic-in-oil mixtures have a separate botulism concern and must never be treated like plain butter at room temperature. Follow a tested recipe and its storage instructions.

Signs that butter should be discarded

Throw it away when it has:

  • Mold.
  • Unusual surface growth.
  • Sour, paint-like, stale, metallic, or soapy odor.
  • Contamination from raw food.
  • Insects or debris.
  • Unknown storage history.
  • A clearly abnormal appearance for that product.

Rancidity is often a quality problem rather than an acute pathogen signal, but there is little value in preserving butter that smells or tastes like an old crayon.

During a power outage

Federal outage guidance lists butter among the foods that can generally be kept after refrigerator temperatures rise, unlike most perishable dairy products. [2]

Inspect for contamination, package damage, and rancidity. Floodwater, sewage, smoke, or chemical exposure changes the decision.

Bottom line

Keep ordinary commercial butter covered at cool room temperature for no more than one to two days.

Leave out only a small portion and refrigerate the rest. Follow stricter instructions for whipped, reduced-fat, homemade, raw-milk, or flavored butter.

Limits and cautions

  • The one-to-two-day guidance applies to ordinary butter, not every dairy spread or compound butter.
  • A warm kitchen shortens useful quality compared with a cool stable room.
  • Package instructions remain controlling when they require refrigeration.
