How to Choose a Portable Induction Cooktop

Portable induction cooktops heat magnetic cookware directly. The pan must be compatible, reasonably flat, and matched to the active coil area.

Updated Aug 5, 2026 4 min read Researched Not hands-on tested
Disclosure

This article contains no paid or affiliate links.

Read the disclosure

What to know

  • Portable induction cooktops heat magnetic cookware directly. The pan must be compatible, reasonably flat, and matched to the active coil area.
  • Choose from cookware compatibility, coil size, electrical load, low-power control, noise, ventilation, and certification.

Start with four questions:

  1. What cookware will be used?
  2. What electrical circuit is available?
  3. Is low-power simmering important?
  4. What pan diameter must heat evenly?

Then compare:

  • Maximum watts
  • Power increments
  • Temperature controls
  • Coil or cooking-zone size
  • Timer
  • Fan noise
  • Control lock
  • Pan detection
  • Certification
  • Warranty

DOE explains that induction cooking uses an electromagnetic field to heat compatible cookware directly rather than heating an exposed resistance element first.

Test the cookware

A simple screen is the magnet test.

Place a magnet against the bottom of the pan.

  • Strong attraction usually indicates induction compatibility.
  • Weak or absent attraction suggests poor or no compatibility.

Compatible materials commonly include:

  • Magnetic stainless steel
  • Cast iron
  • Enameled cast iron
  • Carbon steel

Ordinary aluminum, copper, glass, and nonmagnetic stainless steel require a compatible magnetic base.

The magnet should stick across the cooking area, not only to a small decorative disk.

Check pan flatness

Portable induction cooktops work best with a broad flat base.

A warped pan may:

  • Trigger intermittent detection
  • Heat unevenly
  • Vibrate
  • Cycle off
  • Overheat a small contact area

GE advises matching flat cookware to the cooking zone and notes that pan detection can shut induction zones down when suitable cookware is absent.

Compare the active coil size

The glass top may be wider than the electromagnetic coil beneath it.

Check the documented cooking-zone diameter.

A pan much larger than the coil may heat mainly in the center.

That matters for:

  • Wide skillet
  • Griddle
  • Canning vessel
  • Large Dutch oven
  • Sauce reduction

Do not infer coil size from the circle printed on the glass unless the manufacturer says the marking matches the active area.

Maximum wattage

Many full-power countertop models approach 1,800 watts on a 120-volt household circuit.

A representative Duxtop manual specifies a 120-volt, 15-amp supply and maximum output near 1,800 watts.

Do not share the same circuit with another high-power appliance when the combined load could overload it.

Avoid extension cords unless the cooktop manual explicitly approves one.

Power control versus temperature control

Power mode

Controls energy delivered to the cookware.

Useful for:

  • Boiling
  • Sautéing
  • Frying
  • Rapid response

Temperature mode

Uses a sensor and control logic to target a selected surface temperature.

The displayed temperature may not equal the food temperature or every part of the pan.

For precision work, use an appropriate food or surface thermometer.

Low-power performance

Portable units may create low average power by cycling between full power and off.

That can cause a small pot to alternate between:

  • Boiling
  • Stopping
  • Boiling again

Look for:

  • Fine watt increments
  • Stable low-output operation
  • Manufacturer explanation
  • Independent testing where available

A long list of temperature buttons does not prove stable simmer performance.

Fan and cookware noise

Normal sounds can include:

  • Cooling fan
  • Soft buzzing
  • Clicking
  • Pan vibration

Noise varies with:

  • Pan construction
  • Power level
  • Base flatness
  • Layered metal
  • Fan design

Stop using the unit for:

  • Electrical arcing
  • Burning odor
  • Grinding fan
  • Melted plug
  • Repeated breaker trip
  • Cracked glass

Safety features

Useful features include:

  • No-pan shutoff
  • Overheat protection
  • Timer
  • Control lock
  • Hot-surface indicator
  • Automatic shutoff
  • Small-object rejection

GE documents pan detection and automatic zone shutoff as ordinary induction safety and operating features.

These controls do not make unattended cooking safe.

Certification

Look for:

  • Recognized electrical certification
  • Exact manufacturer
  • Exact model
  • Published manual
  • Warranty
  • Replacement support
  • Recall history

UL’s household electric-cooking-appliance standards cover relevant safety construction and testing for listed products.

A generic marketplace claim saying “FCC certified” is not a substitute for appliance electrical-safety certification.

Ventilation

Induction creates less waste heat at the element than conventional resistance or gas cooking, but the food can still produce:

  • Smoke
  • Grease aerosol
  • Steam
  • Combustion particles from overheated oil

Use appropriate ventilation.

Keep the cooktop’s cooling intake and exhaust openings clear.

Do not operate it on a towel, bed, carpet, or another soft surface that blocks airflow.

Medical implants

Induction cooking generates electromagnetic fields.

People with pacemakers, implanted defibrillators, or other sensitive medical devices should follow the device manufacturer and clinician’s guidance on separation and use.

Do not rely on a general internet distance applied to every implant model.

Bottom line

Choose from cookware compatibility, coil size, electrical load, low-power control, noise, ventilation, and certification.

Induction is fast and responsive, but the printed glass circle, displayed temperature, and maximum wattage each describe only part of what the cooktop will do to an actual pan.
