How to Check for Recalls Before Buying a Used Product

Find the exact model, serial number, production date, lot code, or VIN and search the appropriate official regulator and manufacturer before buying. Confirm that any required repair, replacement, refund, or stop-use remedy was actually completed.

Updated Aug 2, 2026 4 min read Researched Testing not applicable
Disclosure

This article contains no paid or affiliate links.

Read the disclosure

What to know

  • Marketplace titles are not precise enough; use the product label or VIN.
  • Use CPSC for most household products, NHTSA for vehicles and related equipment, FDA for regulated health products, and USDA FSIS for covered meat and poultry recalls.
  • A seller's statement that a recall was fixed should be supported by records tied to the exact product.

Before buying a used product, locate its identifying label and search for recalls.

Do not rely only on the seller saying it has never caused a problem. Recalls concern known hazards or safety defects, not merely whether one particular unit has already failed.

CPSC says selling a recalled consumer product is unlawful unless an approved remedy has been completed where applicable. [1]

Information to collect

Photograph or write down the manufacturer, brand, full model number, serial number, manufacturing date or date code, lot or batch number, vehicle identification number when applicable, product type, and any certification labels.

Search the complete model number rather than a shortened marketplace title. A listing may say only “compact heater,” while the recall applies to one exact model and date range. The label is the useful evidence.

Choose the correct recall database

Household consumer products

Search the Consumer Product Safety Commission for appliances, furniture, electronics, power tools, toys, exercise equipment, baby products, and many other household goods. Individual notices describe affected models, hazards, incidents, remedies, and consumer contacts. [2]

Vehicles, car seats, tires, and vehicle equipment

Use the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. A VIN search can identify unrepaired safety recalls for participating manufacturers. Searches by year, make, and model provide broader recall and manufacturer-communication results. [3]

A car seat, tire, trailer, or other piece of vehicle equipment may require a product search rather than the vehicle VIN.

Food, medicine, cosmetics, and medical products

Search FDA recall and safety-alert resources. FDA notes that not every recall receives a press release, so older or less visible products may require its archives or enforcement reports. [4]

Meat, poultry, and processed egg products

Search USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service recalls and public-health alerts. [5]

For a durable household purchase, CPSC or NHTSA will usually be the starting point. Perishable food should not ordinarily be purchased through an informal used marketplace, for reasons civilization should not need footnoted.

Search more than one way

Try the exact model number, brand and product type, serial-number range, model number plus the word “recall,” manufacturer recall page, alternate formatting, and older corporate names.

Verify every result against the official notice. A forum post, marketplace warning, or search snippet is not the final source.

Read the entire notice

Confirm the exact models, serial or date-code range, product photographs, hazard, reported incidents, required remedy, stop-use instructions, manufacturer contact, and whether the remedy remains available.

A repair kit offered years ago may not be administered the same way today. Confirm current instructions with the manufacturer.

Ask for proof that the remedy was completed

Useful evidence includes a manufacturer repair receipt, dealer service record, replacement confirmation, photograph of an installed remedy, updated serial record, or written confirmation tied to the VIN or serial number.

CPSC says a recalled product may be sold after the prescribed remedy has been applied, and the listing should clearly disclose that fact. [1]

Walk away when

Do not buy when the identification label is missing or removed, the seller refuses to provide the model or serial number, a stop-use recall has no documented remedy, the remedy requires destruction or refund rather than repair, safety features were bypassed, wiring or batteries are damaged, or a safety-critical product is too old to identify reliably.

A bargain is not improved by adding an undocumented fire, tip-over, strangulation, or crash hazard.

After buying

Register the product with the manufacturer when possible and subscribe to recall alerts. Regulators continue issuing recalls after products enter use. Keep photographs of the label and purchase record because labels can fade or become inaccessible after installation.

Bottom line

Find the exact identifier, search the appropriate official regulator, read the complete notice, and verify that any required remedy was completed.

No label means no reliable recall check. For safety-critical products, that is a strong reason not to buy.

Limits and cautions

  • A clean search does not guarantee that no future recall will be issued.
  • Recall databases and remedy availability can change, so status should be rechecked immediately before purchase.
