Are Extended Warranties on Appliances Worth It?

Read the complete contract before paying. Compare coverage dates, parts, labor, deductibles, service fees, shipping, food-loss coverage, reimbursement caps, replacement rules, maintenance duties, and the company responsible for claims.

An appliance “extended warranty” is usually a separately purchased service contract, not the written warranty included with the appliance.

It may be useful when it adds meaningful coverage at a reasonable price.

It may be poor value when it duplicates existing protection or excludes the failures most likely to matter.

Quick answer: Read the complete contract before paying. Compare coverage dates, parts, labor, deductibles, service fees, shipping, food-loss coverage, reimbursement caps, replacement rules, maintenance duties, and the company responsible for claims.

Warranty versus service contract

FTC distinguishes:

  • Written warranty included with the product
  • Implied warranty under state law
  • Separately purchased service contract[1][2]

A service contract costs extra.

Do not call it free manufacturer coverage.

Check existing protection

Before buying, review:

  • Manufacturer warranty
  • Retailer return period
  • Credit-card benefit
  • Homeowner or renter insurance
  • Utility protection plan
  • Existing home service contract
  • Consumer law
  • State implied warranty

Coverage may overlap.

A contract that starts immediately can waste part of its term duplicating the manufacturer warranty.

Length and start date

Ask:

  • When does coverage begin?
  • When does it end?
  • Is the term measured from purchase or warranty expiration?
  • Does it include waiting period?
  • Does replacement end the contract?
  • Is coverage transferable?

Write the dates.

Do not rely on “five years of protection” without understanding what those years include.

Covered failures

Check exact coverage for:

  • Parts
  • labor
  • electronics
  • sealed system
  • compressor
  • motor
  • heating element
  • cosmetic damage
  • accidental damage
  • food loss
  • power surge
  • rust
  • scale
  • normal wear
  • accessories

If it is not written, assume it is not covered.

Exclusions

Common exclusions may involve:

  • Improper installation
  • Commercial use
  • normal maintenance
  • cosmetic damage
  • preexisting condition
  • rust or corrosion
  • pest damage
  • water quality
  • misuse
  • power issue
  • unauthorized repair
  • failure to clean
  • inaccessible installation
  • consequential damage

A broad exclusion can matter more than the headline coverage.

Deductibles and fees

Include:

  • Service-call fee
  • deductible
  • shipping
  • diagnostic fee
  • removal
  • installation
  • haul-away
  • mileage
  • transfer fee
  • cancellation fee

A “covered” repair with a $150 service fee is not free.

Reimbursement limits

Check:

  • Per-claim cap
  • annual cap
  • lifetime cap
  • depreciated value
  • purchase-price cap
  • food-loss cap
  • replacement value
  • cash settlement
  • gift-card settlement

A refrigerator contract capped at a small depreciated amount may not replace the appliance.

Claims process

Ask:

  • Who administers the contract?
  • Who chooses the technician?
  • Must authorization happen before repair?
  • How fast is service?
  • Is reimbursement allowed?
  • Must the appliance be shipped?
  • Is there local coverage?
  • What records are required?
  • How is a dispute handled?

FTC warns that difficult claims and reimbursement processes reduce value.[2]

Provider reputation

The seller may not be the company responsible for coverage.

Research:

  • Legal company name
  • years in business
  • state registration
  • complaint history
  • financial backing
  • cancellation
  • insolvency protection
  • administrator

A point-of-sale salesperson’s confidence is not claims capacity.

Self-insurance

Instead of buying the contract, a household can save the same amount in a repair fund.

Compare:

Contract price
+
expected fees

with:

repair fund

The repair fund:

  • Covers excluded repairs
  • Remains yours if unused
  • Can serve another appliance

The contract:

  • Transfers some covered risk
  • May provide service coordination
  • May cap catastrophic repair exposure

Neither is universally best.

High-cost electronics

Contracts may be more attractive when:

  • Product has expensive proprietary controls
  • Sealed-system coverage is strong
  • Labor is included
  • Local service is available
  • Contract is inexpensive
  • Replacement terms are clear

But exact reliability claims require evidence.

Do not buy from fear generated by the person selling the contract.

Decision checklist

  • Manufacturer warranty read
  • Start and end dates
  • Parts covered
  • Labor covered
  • Deductible
  • Service fee
  • Shipping
  • Installation
  • Replacement standard
  • Reimbursement cap
  • Exclusions
  • Maintenance duties
  • Claims process
  • Administrator
  • Cancellation
  • Credit-card overlap

Frequently asked questions

Is an extended warranty actually a warranty?

Usually it is a separately purchased service contract.

Are service contracts always a scam?

No. Value depends on coverage, cost, claims, and risk.

Does the manufacturer warranty continue?

Read both documents. Terms may overlap.

Can I buy coverage later?

Sometimes, but later offers can be expensive or fraudulent. Verify the company.

What if the company calls saying my warranty is expiring?

FTC warns about high-pressure unsolicited warranty marketing. Do not provide financial information before verifying the company and contract.
