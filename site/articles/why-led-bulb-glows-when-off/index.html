Why Does an LED Bulb Glow Faintly When the Switch Is Off?

A faint LED glow after the switch is off usually means a tiny current is still passing through the lighting circuit. Electronic dimmers, illuminated switches, and locator lights can allow enough leakage current to charge an LED driver even though the current is far too small to visibly light an incandescent bulb.

What to know

  • LED ghosting can be caused by very small leakage current through dimmers or illuminated switches.
  • A locator light or dimmer-bulb incompatibility can keep an LED faintly illuminated after switch-off.
  • Persistent glow on a simple mechanical switch can justify model-specific troubleshooting or an electrician's inspection.

A faint LED glow after the wall switch is off is commonly called ghosting. In many cases, the bulb is not receiving ordinary operating power. Instead, a very small current is still moving through the circuit, and the electronics inside the LED lamp are sensitive enough to respond to it. Lutron documents this behavior with electronic dimmers, and Leviton notes that illuminated switches and locator lights can pass a small amount of current through an LED load even in the off position. [1] [2]

That makes LED ghosting different from the familiar behavior of an incandescent bulb. An incandescent filament needs enough current to heat visibly. An LED driver can react to much smaller currents, so a circuit that looked completely dark with an older bulb may show a faint LED glow after a lamp change.

Why a dimmer can leave an LED faintly on

Many electronic dimmers need a tiny amount of current for their own internal electronics. Depending on the circuit design, some of that current can pass through the connected lamp when the dimmer appears to be off. Lutron describes this as leakage current and notes that it can keep some LED or CFL loads glowing at a low level. [1]

The effect depends on both sides of the circuit. A dimmer may work normally with one LED model but produce ghosting with another because LED drivers are not electrically identical. Lutron’s troubleshooting documentation treats dimmer-and-lamp compatibility as a central factor in off-state glow. [1]

If the glow began immediately after replacing bulbs while the dimmer stayed the same, compatibility is therefore a reasonable first suspect.

Illuminated switches and locator lights can do the same thing

Some switches contain a small locator light so the switch can be found in a dark room. Leviton explains that when the locator light is on, a very small current can pass through the LED bulb. That current can be enough to leave the lamp faintly illuminated. [2]

This also explains why the problem can appear even without a traditional slider or rotary dimmer. A decorative switch with an illuminated locator can still create an off-state current path.

Leviton separately describes LED ghosting as faint illumination caused by leakage current and identifies illuminated-switch designs as a common source. [3]

What to check first

Start with the controls that are easiest to identify.

  1. Confirm whether the switch is a dimmer. Electronic dimmers are a common source of small off-state current.
  2. Look for a locator or night-light feature. If the switch itself glows when the room light is off, its locator circuit may be relevant. [2]
  3. Check the lamp and dimmer compatibility information. Lighting-control manufacturers publish compatibility guidance because LED drivers can behave differently on the same dimmer. [1]
  4. Notice whether every bulb on the circuit glows or only one model does. A model-specific pattern points toward lamp electronics rather than a general lighting failure.
  5. Do not open an energized switch box simply to investigate a faint glow. Circuit-level troubleshooting carries shock and fire risks and should follow normal electrical safety practice.

Is faint LED ghosting dangerous?

The existence of a faint glow alone does not prove that the circuit is unsafe. Manufacturers explicitly document ghosting as a compatibility or leakage-current behavior with certain electronic controls. [1] [3]

But the word ghosting should not become a blanket explanation for every unusual lighting symptom. A switch that is hot, a burning smell, visible arcing, discoloration, repeated breaker trips, or a sudden change in circuit behavior is not merely a cosmetic glow. Those symptoms deserve electrical troubleshooting.

Similarly, a lamp that remains substantially illuminated rather than barely glowing may indicate a different control or wiring condition.

What can reduce the glow?

The correct fix depends on the control system. Manufacturer guidance may suggest disabling a locator-light feature, using a compatible LED lamp, adjusting the control configuration, or using a dimmer designed for the connected LED load. Leviton notes that locator-light current can be responsible for persistent glow, while Lutron documents compatibility-specific ghosting behavior. [2] [1]

Avoid adding improvised resistors, capacitors, or other components inside a line-voltage circuit unless the component and installation method are specifically approved for that application. A faint light is not worth creating an electrical hazard to hide it.

What if there is only a plain mechanical switch?

If the circuit uses a basic mechanical switch with no dimmer, locator light, smart electronics, or other obvious current path, the common manufacturer explanations become less complete. Capacitive coupling between adjacent conductors or another wiring arrangement can sometimes allow very small currents, but determining the actual cause requires knowing the circuit rather than guessing from the bulb.

At that point, especially if the glow is substantial or accompanied by other symptoms, an electrician can measure the circuit and distinguish harmless small-current behavior from a wiring problem.

Bottom line

A faint LED glow after switch-off is often an electronics issue rather than a bulb mysteriously receiving full power. Electronic dimmers and illuminated switches can pass tiny currents that an LED driver responds to, a phenomenon lighting manufacturers call ghosting. [1] [3] Checking the type of switch, locator-light settings, and lamp-dimmer compatibility is the sensible first path. Strong illumination, heat, odor, arcing, or breaker problems are different symptoms and deserve proper electrical diagnosis.

Limits and cautions

  • This guide addresses common LED ghosting documented by lighting-control manufacturers; it does not diagnose a specific building circuit.
  • Electrical wiring conditions vary, and work inside an energized box should be handled only by someone qualified to do so.
  • No direct electrical measurements were performed for this article.
