Why Do Ice Cubes Smell Bad or Taste Strange?

Discard the affected ice, compare the water before freezing, clean the bin and refrigerator, seal strong-smelling food, replace the correct filter when due, and flush new plumbing or filters according to the manual. Stop using the water when the odor suggests contamination or the supply itself is abnormal.

What to know

  • Ice readily picks up odors from uncovered or spoiled food and becomes stale during low use.
  • If tap or dispenser water already tastes wrong, the ice maker is not the first place to look.
  • Chemical, sewage, fuel, or sudden sulfur odors in the water supply need utility or health-authority guidance rather than deodorizing the freezer.

Ice has little flavor of its own, so small odors from freezer air, water, tubing, filters, and stored food become noticeable as it melts. The fastest diagnosis compares fresh water, freshly made ice, and old stored ice.

Discard old ice and clean the bin

Throw away the affected batch. Empty the storage bin and wash it with mild dish detergent and water if the manual allows, then rinse and dry it completely.

Old ice can absorb odors during low use. GE Appliances identifies uncovered food, spilled food, stale ice, water quality, and an old filter among common reasons refrigerator ice tastes bad. [1]

Do not use strong scented cleaner, bleach, or ammonia unless the refrigerator manual specifically approves it. Plastic can retain cleaner odor, and chemical mixing can be dangerous.

Compare the water before freezing

Taste and smell cold tap water and dispenser water separately only when the water supply is otherwise considered safe. If both already have the same abnormal taste, focus on the household water, filter, and plumbing rather than the ice bin.

A sudden chemical, fuel, sewage, solvent, or strong sulfur odor in tap water is not an appliance-cleaning problem. Stop consuming it and contact the water utility, well professional, public health authority, or emergency service as appropriate.

Inspect food and spills

Air circulates between refrigerator and freezer compartments in many models. Open fish, onions, leftovers, spilled milk, decaying produce, and other strong sources can transfer odor to ice.

Discard spoiled food. Clean spills and removable shelves according to the manual. Use airtight, moisture-resistant containers rather than open bowls or loosely folded packaging.

GE also describes a simple comparison test: freeze tap water in a clean tray and store the cubes in the freezer. If they acquire the odor, freezer air is likely involved; if only automatic ice tastes wrong, investigate the water path and ice system. [2]

Check the water filter

Replace the filter at the interval and warning condition specified by the refrigerator manufacturer. Use the exact compatible filter and install it fully. A filter left in service too long can reduce flow and affect taste, but a counterfeit or incorrectly fitted replacement can create its own problems.

Flush the new filter using the model’s instructions. Discard the first batches of ice after installation.

A refrigerator connected without a filter may require a bypass plug. Do not improvise one.

Consider new plumbing and supply tubing

New refrigerators, filters, plastic water lines, and plumbing work can produce temporary tastes. Flush the dispenser and discard early ice according to the installation instructions.

Confirm that the refrigerator is connected to the cold-water line and uses approved tubing. Kinked, contaminated, or unsuitable tubing can affect flow and taste.

Check for sanitation problems

Persistent slime, mold-like growth, insects, standing water, or odor inside inaccessible ice-maker parts may require manufacturer service. Do not dismantle electrical or refrigeration components to clean them.

For a refrigerator that lost power, flooded, or contained spoiled food for an extended period, follow authoritative food-safety and appliance-cleaning guidance before returning it to service.

Make a fresh comparison batch

After cleaning, flushing, and correcting food storage, allow the machine to make new ice. Compare the first discarded batch, the next fresh batch, and water from the supply.

The pattern identifies the likely source: bad water affects both water and ice; freezer odor affects tray ice too; an automatic-ice-only problem points toward the filter, tubing, bin, or ice-maker system.

Limits and cautions

  • Odor and taste can originate in source water, plumbing, filter media, supply tubing, refrigerator materials, stored food, cleaning chemicals, or microbial growth; smell alone cannot identify the cause.
